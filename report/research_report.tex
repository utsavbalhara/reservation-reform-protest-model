\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=12pt]
\item An abrupt switch draws about \PeakCentralBaseline{} onto the streets on the peak day (\CumulativeCentralBaseline{} ever protest, about \DeathsCentralBaseline{} deaths).

\item Compensating the people who lose eligibility does little (\PeakChangeCentralCompensation{}).
\item Internet shutdowns and heavy policing lower the peak modestly while multiplying deaths by \DeathRatioCentralInternetShutdown{} and \DeathRatioCentralHeavyPolicing{} times.
\end{itemize}

\section{The question}

India reserves seats in public education and government jobs for Scheduled Castes (SC, 15\%), Scheduled Tribes (ST, 7.5\%) and Other Backward Classes (OBC, 27\%). Since 2019 it has also reserved 10\% for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) of the general category. Two of these categories already carry an income test. OBC candidates from families above \rupee8 lakh a year (the ``creamy layer'') are excluded, and EWS applies only below \rupee8 lakh. SC and ST quotas have no income test at all.

This report asks what would happen if the whole system were converted into a single income test at \rupee8 lakh, with caste removed as a criterion. It asks two questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How many people would protest, and for how long?
\item Which changes to the policy's design, sequencing and politics would keep that protest small?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Who actually changes status}
At the \rupee8 lakh line the eligibility change is small. OBC families above the line and general-category families below it already have the status an income test would give them. The people who lose eligibility are SC and ST families above \rupee8 lakh, about 2.8\% of the population in this model (roughly 4 crore people). About 80\% of Indians are reserved before and after, and about 17\% are unreserved before and after.

That small eligibility shift is why protest is a puzzle worth modelling. Recent history shows that reservation politics mobilizes around what a change symbolizes, not only around who loses a benefit:
\begin{itemize}
\item In 2018 a Supreme Court ruling that diluted the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act removed nobody's quota, yet it triggered the largest Bharat Bandh on record and at least 11 deaths.
\item In 2024 the Supreme Court permitted sub-classification within SC/ST quotas, and some judges suggested a creamy layer for them. That triggered another nationwide bandh.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theory: why people would protest}

Five bodies of theory shape the model. Each is stated formally, then in plain words.

\subsection{Loss aversion and the politics of taking things away}
People weigh losses roughly twice as heavily as equal gains; Tversky and Kahneman estimate a loss-aversion coefficient of about 2.25. Pierson's work on welfare retrenchment builds on this. Cutting an existing benefit is politically harder than creating one, because the losses are concentrated, immediate and visible while the gains are diffuse and uncertain. Governments that succeed at retrenchment typically use three techniques:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Grandfathering}, which exempts people already receiving the benefit.
\item \emph{Compensation} for the people who lose.
\item \emph{Blame-sharing} through broad coalitions.
\end{itemize}
\begin{plainwords}Taking away a quota feels worse than never having had one, and the people who lose are easier to organize than the people who gain.\end{plainwords}

\subsection{Symbolic threat and group identity}
Social identity theory holds that people derive part of their self-worth from group membership, and react strongly to threats to the group's status. For SC and ST communities, caste-based reservation is also a constitutional recognition of historical injustice. Removing caste as the basis therefore carries a symbolic threat independent of any individual's eligibility. The 2018 bandh is the clearest Indian evidence that symbolic threat alone can mobilize at national scale.
\begin{plainwords}Many people would protest not because they lose a seat, but because they read the change as erasing their community's recognition.\end{plainwords}

\subsection{Threshold cascades}
Granovetter's threshold model treats each person as having a personal threshold: the share of others who must already be protesting before they join. A few activists join at once, and their presence tips people with slightly higher thresholds, and so on. Small differences in the distribution of thresholds or grievances can produce very large differences in turnout, which makes the system non-linear near its tipping point.
\begin{plainwords}People join when enough of their neighbours already have. Pushing grievance down a little can remove far more protest than you would expect, because it stops the chain reaction early.\end{plainwords}

\subsection{Organization and elite cues}
Resource-mobilization theory (McCarthy and Zald) argues that grievance is common but organized protest is not. Organizations, parties and networks supply the buses, bandh calls and messaging that convert grievance into turnout. Both the 2018 and 2024 bandhs had support from opposition parties.
\begin{plainwords}Anger needs machinery. When opposition parties co-own a reform, they stop supplying that machinery to protests against it.\end{plainwords}

\subsection{Repression and backfire}
Studies of repression find that visible force against protesters often backfires. Deaths create martyrs, and perceived injustice broadens support. India has imposed more internet shutdowns than any other country, and Rydzak finds that shutdowns in India are associated with more violent collective action, not less, because they disrupt the coordination that peaceful protest relies on.
\begin{plainwords}Cutting the internet or sending in heavy policing may shrink the crowd on a given day, but it tends to make protest angrier, longer and deadlier.\end{plainwords}

\subsection{The synthetic India}
The model builds 120{,}000 agents, each standing for about 12{,}000 people. Group shares follow the 2011 Census for SC (16.6\%) and ST (8.6\%) and survey estimates for OBC (42\%), leaving 32.8\% General. About one family in five sits above \rupee8 lakh: 30\% of General, 17\% of OBC, 12\% of SC and 10\% of ST. SC and ST are split into a better-off tier and a most-deprived tier, with most above-line families in the better-off tier. Agents live in neighbourhoods of about 100 agents from the same group.

\subsection{How one day works}
For every agent, every day:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Grievance} is the material loss (weighted 2.25 times if it is a loss) plus the symbolic threat multiplied by how strongly the agent identifies with its group.
\item \textbf{Social pull} rises with the share of the agent's neighbourhood that protested yesterday and with its group's national turnout. Both effects saturate, because a crowd can only pull so hard.
\item \textbf{Organization} adds a push on bandh-call days (days 3, 13 and 26), larger when opposition parties back the protest.
\item The agent \textbf{protests} with a probability that rises smoothly as grievance plus pull plus push exceeds its personal threshold.
\item Protesting raises the agent's threshold a little (\textbf{fatigue}).
\item Deaths occur in proportion to turnout. Each death raises symbolic threat for the groups on the street (the \textbf{martyr effect}).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Formally, agent $i$ in group $g$ protests on day $t$ with probability
\begin{equation}
P_{i,t}=\sigma\!\left(\frac{w_m\,L(m_i)+w_s\,\iota_i\,(s_g+\mu_{g,t})+\beta_n\tanh\!\frac{n_{c(i),t-1}}{n^*}+\beta_v\tanh\!\frac{v_{g,t-1}}{v^*}+a\,o_g\,b_t-\theta_{i,t}}{\tau}\right),
\end{equation}
where $L(m)=\lambda m$ for losses and $m$ for gains, $\iota_i$ is identity strength, $s_g$ symbolic threat, $\mu_{g,t}$ the martyr effect, $n$ and $v$ neighbourhood and national turnout, $o_g$ organizational capacity, $a$ the opposition-party amplifier, $b_t$ the bandh-day mobilization, and $\theta_{i,t}$ the agent's threshold.

\subsection{Calibration and a validation check}
The one free level parameter is the mean participation threshold. It is set to 6.6, which puts the baseline peak near 46 lakh, the middle of an independent prior estimate of 20 lakh to 1 crore. The check that matters is separate. Removing all material loss leaves only symbolic threat, much like the 2018 Atrocities Act ruling. That shock alone should still produce a bandh at 2018 scale. The model gives \PeakCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation{}, which passes.

\section{Scenario by scenario}

Each scenario below follows the same layout: what the policy is, the theory behind it, a plain-language summary, how it enters the model, the outcome, and why the outcome came out that way. All percentages compare peak-day turnout with the baseline median of \PeakCentralBaseline{}.

\subsection{Base: the abrupt \rupee8 lakh income-only switch}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Parliament replaces SC, ST, OBC and EWS quotas with a single income test at \rupee8 lakh, effective immediately, with no consultation or transition.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Every protest driver is at full strength. Above-line SC and ST families suffer a concentrated loss; below-line reserved groups lose their separate pools; symbolic threat is unmitigated; and opposition parties are free to mobilize.
\begin{plainwords}This is the reference point: the reform done in the most provocative way.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralBaseline}{\PeakRangeCentralBaseline}{--}{\CumulativeCentralBaseline}{\DeathsCentralBaseline}{\PeakHighBaseline{} peak, \CumulativeHighBaseline{} ever protest, \DeathsHighBaseline{} deaths.}
\subsubsection{Why}
SC communities carry most of the turnout (Figure~\ref{fig:groups}): they have the highest symbolic threat, the strongest organizations and the largest group of direct losers. Turnout spikes on each bandh call and decays between them as fatigue sets in. The wide range across runs reflects how close the system sits to its tipping point.

\caption{Baseline turnout by group. SC turnout dominates; General-category turnout is zero because the reform favours them.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{V: validation, symbolic threat only}
\subsubsection{What it is}
A check, not a policy. All material loss is removed, so only the symbolic threat of abolishing caste-based quotas remains. This resembles the 2018 Atrocities Act ruling, which changed no one's quota.
\subsubsection{Theory}
If identity-based mobilization is real, a symbolic shock alone should still produce a large national bandh.
\begin{plainwords}We checked that the model can reproduce something like the 2018 bandh before trusting it on anything else.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation}{\PeakRangeCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation}{\PeakChangeCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation}{\CumulativeCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation}{\DeathsCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation}{\PeakHighSymbolicOnlyValidation{} peak.}
\subsubsection{Why}
Symbolic threat alone produces bandh-scale protest, consistent with 2018. Removing material loss still cuts turnout by about 70\%, because material loss pushes the most motivated agents over their thresholds and they seed the cascade.

\subsection{L1: grandfather current cohorts, with a 10-year glide}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Students and job applicants already in the pipeline keep their current category rights. The income test is phased in over ten years.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Loss aversion applies to losses felt now. Grandfathering is Pierson's classic blame-avoidance tool: it converts an immediate, concentrated loss into a deferred and discounted one.
\begin{plainwords}Nobody who is counting on a quota today loses it, and the change arrives slowly enough to plan around.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Every material loss is cut to 30\% of its baseline value; symbolic threat falls by 5\%.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralGrandfathering}{\PeakRangeCentralGrandfathering}{\PeakChangeCentralGrandfathering}{\CumulativeCentralGrandfathering}{\DeathsCentralGrandfathering}{\PeakHighGrandfathering{} (\PeakChangeHighGrandfathering).}
\subsubsection{Why}
Grandfathering takes out most of the material component, so turnout falls almost to the symbolic-only level (\PeakCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation). What remains is protest about recognition, which grandfathering does not address.

\subsection{L2: expand seats so no group loses}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Seats and posts are expanded so that no group's absolute number of seats falls, as the government did in 2019 when it added about 2.14 lakh seats in central institutions to introduce EWS without cutting other quotas.
\subsubsection{Theory}
When reserved pools merge, below-line SC, ST and OBC candidates face more competition. Expanding capacity removes that loss for the large below-line majority.
\begin{plainwords}If the pie grows, nobody's slice has to shrink.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Material loss for below-line SC, ST and OBC is set to zero. Above-line SC and ST still lose eligibility.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralSeatExpansion}{\PeakRangeCentralSeatExpansion}{\PeakChangeCentralSeatExpansion}{\CumulativeCentralSeatExpansion}{\DeathsCentralSeatExpansion}{\PeakHighSeatExpansion{} (\PeakChangeHighSeatExpansion).}
\subsubsection{Why}
The below-line loss is spread thinly across many people, so removing it lowers grievance only modestly per person. Both the direct losers and the symbolic threat remain. It is a useful supporting measure, not a solution.

\subsection{L3: keep caste sub-quotas, add an income filter inside them}
\subsubsection{What it is}
A hybrid design. SC, ST and OBC quotas stay, but within each quota only families below the income line are eligible. In effect this extends the OBC creamy layer to SC and ST. Brazil's 2012 Quota Law works similarly: racial sub-quotas sit inside a public-school and income filter.
\subsubsection{Theory}
It keeps caste recognition, which carries most of the symbolic threat, while still targeting benefits by income. It also avoids merging pools, so below-line candidates lose nothing.
\begin{plainwords}Keep the caste categories, but stop the richest families inside them from using the quota.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Symbolic threat for SC, ST and OBC falls to 40\% of baseline; below-line material loss is removed. Above-line SC and ST still lose eligibility.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralHybridCasteSubquotas}{\PeakRangeCentralHybridCasteSubquotas}{\PeakChangeCentralHybridCasteSubquotas}{\CumulativeCentralHybridCasteSubquotas}{\DeathsCentralHybridCasteSubquotas}{\PeakHighHybridCasteSubquotas{} (\PeakChangeHighHybridCasteSubquotas).}
\subsubsection{Why}
It attacks the largest driver directly, and the cascade does the rest: once most agents fall below threshold, the chain reaction never starts. Remaining protest comes from above-line SC and ST families, who still lose. The result holds even if the hybrid removes only a fifth of the symbolic threat (\HybridRetainedEighty{}; Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). The main caution is the 2024 bandh, which a mere suggestion of an SC/ST creamy layer triggered. The real symbolic relief may be at the weak end of the tested range. This design also gives up the premise of the question, since caste stays a criterion.

\subsection{L4: sub-classify to favour the most-deprived}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Within SC and ST, a larger share of benefits goes to the most-deprived sub-castes, as permitted by the Supreme Court in 2024.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Coalitions can split when a reform creates real winners inside a protesting group. During the 2024 bandh, about 30 SC/ST communities in Rajasthan opposed the protest because they supported sub-classification.
\begin{plainwords}Give the poorest sub-castes a better deal, and many of them will not join the protest.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
The most-deprived tier gains (material loss reduced by 0.5) and feels only 30\% of the symbolic threat.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralSubClassification}{\PeakRangeCentralSubClassification}{\PeakChangeCentralSubClassification}{\CumulativeCentralSubClassification}{\DeathsCentralSubClassification}{\PeakHighSubClassification{} (\PeakChangeHighSubClassification).}
\subsubsection{Why}
The most-deprived tier largely drops out, but the better-off tier, which includes nearly all the direct losers, keeps protesting. Many neighbourhoods are mixed across tiers, so the better-off tier's cascade still runs.

\subsection{L5: data-first commission with cross-party consensus}
\subsubsection{What it is}
The reform goes through a statutory commission that works from the caste census data before any legislation, with opposition parties represented and the final design negotiated across parties.
\subsubsection{Theory}
It combines procedural legitimacy with Pierson's blame-sharing. When opposition parties co-own a reform, they do not supply the organization, messaging and bandh calls that turn grievance into turnout.
\begin{plainwords}If every major party signs off, nobody has a reason to bus people into the streets against it.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
The opposition-party amplifier falls from 1.5 to 1.0; symbolic threat falls by 20\%.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralConsensusCommission}{\PeakRangeCentralConsensusCommission}{\PeakChangeCentralConsensusCommission}{\CumulativeCentralConsensusCommission}{\DeathsCentralConsensusCommission}{\PeakHighConsensusCommission{} (\PeakChangeHighConsensusCommission).}
\subsubsection{Why}
Bandh-day mobilization sets peak turnout, and consensus removes a third of it while lowering symbolic threat for everyone. This is the strongest lever that keeps the full income-only end state, and it is the load-bearing part of the managed-transition package. It is also the hardest to achieve in practice.

\subsection{L6: compensate above-line losers}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Families that lose eligibility receive scholarships, hostel places and transitional grants.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Pierson's compensation strategy: buy off the concentrated losers.
\begin{plainwords}Pay the people who lose the quota.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Material loss for above-line SC and ST is halved.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralCompensation}{\PeakRangeCentralCompensation}{\PeakChangeCentralCompensation}{\CumulativeCentralCompensation}{\DeathsCentralCompensation}{\PeakHighCompensation{} (\PeakChangeHighCompensation).}
\subsubsection{Why}
The direct losers are under 3\% of the population, and money does nothing about symbolic threat. Compensation is the weakest lever, and removing it from the managed-transition package barely changes that package's result.

\subsection{L7: guarantee untouched protections}
\subsubsection{What it is}
The reform is written with explicit guarantees about what stays untouched: SC/ST reserved seats in legislatures, the Atrocities Act, and a scheduled review clause.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Symbolic threat depends on what people believe the reform will lead to next. Credible guarantees limit that fear.
\begin{plainwords}Say clearly, in law, what is not changing.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Symbolic threat falls by 15\% for every group.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralCredibleGuarantees}{\PeakRangeCentralCredibleGuarantees}{\PeakChangeCentralCredibleGuarantees}{\CumulativeCentralCredibleGuarantees}{\DeathsCentralCredibleGuarantees}{\PeakHighCredibleGuarantees{} (\PeakChangeHighCredibleGuarantees).}
\subsubsection{Why}
A 15\% cut in symbolic threat produces a much larger cut in turnout. Symbolic threat is the dominant driver, and the cascade multiplies any reduction.

\subsection{B1: internet shutdowns (tested for backfire)}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Mobile internet is suspended in areas with protests, as Indian states have frequently done.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Rydzak finds that shutdowns in India are associated with more violent collective action, because they disrupt the coordination that disciplined, peaceful protest needs.
\begin{plainwords}Cutting the internet makes it harder to organize, but it makes what does happen more chaotic and violent.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Neighbourhood and national influence fall by 40\% and bandh mobilization by 20\%, while the death rate rises 2.5 times.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralInternetShutdown}{\PeakRangeCentralInternetShutdown}{\PeakChangeCentralInternetShutdown}{\CumulativeCentralInternetShutdown{} (\CumulativeChangeCentralInternetShutdown)}{\DeathsCentralInternetShutdown{} (×\DeathRatioCentralInternetShutdown)}{\PeakHighInternetShutdown{} (\PeakChangeHighInternetShutdown), \DeathsHighInternetShutdown{} deaths (×\DeathRatioHighInternetShutdown).}
\subsubsection{Why}
Weaker coordination trims the peak, and more so when mobilization is high. But extra deaths feed the martyr effect, which raises symbolic threat and keeps people coming out, so more people protest overall in the central regime. Every version of this lever costs lives.

\subsection{B2: heavy policing and mass arrests (tested for backfire)}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Large deployments, preventive detentions and forceful dispersal on bandh days.
\subsubsection{Theory}
Force raises the cost of turning out but produces martyrs and perceived injustice, which broadens support.
\begin{plainwords}Force can shrink a crowd on the day, but every death recruits more protesters for the days after.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{How it is modelled}
Turning out on bandh days costs 0.4 more; the death rate triples; each death raises symbolic threat 1.5 times as much.
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralHeavyPolicing}{\PeakRangeCentralHeavyPolicing}{\PeakChangeCentralHeavyPolicing}{\CumulativeCentralHeavyPolicing{} (\CumulativeChangeCentralHeavyPolicing)}{\DeathsCentralHeavyPolicing{} (×\DeathRatioCentralHeavyPolicing)}{\PeakHighHeavyPolicing{} (\PeakChangeHighHeavyPolicing), \DeathsHighHeavyPolicing{} deaths (×\DeathRatioHighHeavyPolicing).}
\subsubsection{Why}
Figure~\ref{fig:paths} shows the mechanism. Policing flattens the bandh-day spikes, but the martyr effect keeps more people on the street between bandhs, so more people protest in total and many more die.

\subsection{C1: the managed-transition package}
\subsubsection{What it is}
Keep the full income-only end state, but get there with grandfathering (L1), seat expansion (L2), a consensus commission (L5), compensation (L6) and guarantees (L7).
\subsubsection{Theory}
Each lever pushes agents further below threshold. Because turnout responds non-linearly, combined levers do better than their separate effects suggest.
\begin{plainwords}Do the same reform, but slowly, with more seats, with every party on board, and with clear promises.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralManagedTransition}{\PeakRangeCentralManagedTransition}{\PeakChangeCentralManagedTransition}{\CumulativeCentralManagedTransition}{\DeathsCentralManagedTransition}{\PeakHighManagedTransition{} (\PeakChangeHighManagedTransition).}
\subsubsection{Why}
The package stays below the tipping point in both regimes. Leaving out the consensus commission triples turnout (\ManagedTransitionWithoutBuildConsensusThroughDataFirstCommission{} against \ManagedTransitionFull{}), while leaving out compensation barely matters (\ManagedTransitionWithoutCompensateAboveLineLosers{}).

\subsection{C2: the hybrid-design package}
\subsubsection{What it is}
The hybrid design (L3) with sub-classification (L4), grandfathering (L1), the consensus commission (L5), compensation (L6) and guarantees (L7).
\begin{plainwords}Keep caste categories with an income filter, favour the poorest sub-castes, phase it in, and get every party on board.\end{plainwords}
\subsubsection{Outcome}
\outcomeline{\PeakCentralHybridPackage}{\PeakRangeCentralHybridPackage}{\PeakChangeCentralHybridPackage}{\CumulativeCentralHybridPackage}{\DeathsCentralHybridPackage}{\PeakHighHybridPackage{} (\PeakChangeHighHybridPackage).}
\subsubsection{Why}
Almost every driver is addressed at once. What remains is roughly the protest an ordinary contested policy change would draw.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
Two checks test whether the headline findings rest on a single assumption.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Hybrid sensitivity.} The hybrid was rerun leaving 40\%, 60\% and 80\% of symbolic threat in place. Peak turnout was \HybridRetainedForty{}, \HybridRetainedSixty{} and \HybridRetainedEighty{}: still at least an 80\% cut.
\item \textbf{Managed-transition leave-one-out.} Each of the five components was removed in turn. Only removing the consensus commission changes the result materially.
\end{itemize}
The ranking of levers is the same in the central and high-mobilization regimes.

\section{What this means}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Design matters more than compensation.} Protest against this reform is mostly about recognition, so a design that preserves caste recognition while targeting by income does far better than paying the people who lose.
\item \textbf{Process is policy.} If the income-only end state is the goal, a data-first commission with cross-party ownership matters more than any single material concession.
\item \textbf{Suppression is not a mitigation strategy.} In this model it lowers peak turnout only modestly while multiplying deaths, consistent with India's own record on shutdowns.
\item \textbf{Levers compound.} Because turnout responds non-linearly, packages do far better than their parts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item Grievance weights, lever effects and neighbourhood structure are assumptions informed by qualitative evidence, not estimates from Indian protest data.
\item The calibration target is itself an estimate.
\item Regional variation, counter-protests, courts, elections and media are not modelled.
\item Neighbourhoods are single-group. Real mixing across groups could either dampen or spread protest.
\item The model runs 40 days and says nothing about long-term political consequences.
\end{itemize}
Treat magnitudes as order-of-magnitude and the ranking as the finding.
