\begin{abstract}
\noindent A recurring proposal in India is to replace caste-based reservation with a single income test at \rupee8 lakh. We ask how much street protest such a reform would provoke and which designs would keep it small, and we build the evidence base to answer as far as data allow. Three linked models do the work. An eligibility accounting under the actual creamy-layer and EWS rules finds that \EligibilityLose{} of Indians would lose eligibility and \EligibilityGain{} would gain it. An allocation model built on India's over-and-above choice rule and five years of JEE (Advanced) rank lists finds that a merged income pool would cost below-line SC candidates \AllocChangeSCBelow{} of their IIT seats although they keep eligibility. An agent-based model of 640 Census districts, history-matched to protest event counts from four episodes, turns these losses and the symbolic threat to caste recognition into protest. The event data pin down the relative size of past shocks far better than turnout, and frozen out-of-sample predictions of deaths and geography are weak, so the reform's protest level is not identified: it spans more than an order of magnitude across the assumptions the data leave open. The comparison of designs is much more stable. Keeping caste quotas with an income filter inside them changes peak turnout by \PeakChangeGroundedHybridCasteSubquotas{} and is the strongest lever in every structural variant and assumption we test, and with probability \MapGroundedStrongestLThree{} under priors on intervention effects. Compensating direct losers and expanding seats on the EWS model are the weakest. Whether a consensus commission or grandfathering is the better end-state-preserving lever is not settled, and suppression costs lives whether or not it backfires.
\end{abstract}

\noindent{\small\textbf{Keywords:} reservation policy; affirmative action; protest; agent-based modelling; history matching; event data; matching markets; India}

\section{Introduction}

Few Indian policies generate as much contention as reservation. Quotas for Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST) and Other Backward Classes (OBC) are defended as constitutional remedies for historical exclusion and criticized as caste-based rather than need-based. A proposal that surfaces repeatedly in public debate is to replace caste categories with a single income criterion. Its supporters present it as fairer; its opponents present it as the end of caste-based recognition.

This paper does not take a position on whether that reform is desirable. It asks two narrower questions that any government contemplating it would need answered:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Q\arabic*.}]
\item How much street protest would an income-only reform provoke, and from whom?
\item Which choices of policy design, sequencing and political process would keep that protest small, and which commonly used responses would make it worse?
\end{enumerate}

These questions are hard to answer from data alone. The reform has never been attempted, protest turnout in India is poorly measured, and the mechanisms involved interact non-linearly: loss aversion, group identity, contagion between neighbours, organization and repression. An agent-based model makes the assumed mechanisms explicit and lets alternative designs be compared under identical conditions \citep{epstein2002,granovetter1978}. It also makes it easy to mistake assumptions for findings. Much of this paper is therefore about separating the two: which inputs can be grounded in data, which conclusions follow from the dynamics rather than from how an intervention was coded, and which survive changes to the model's structure.

\paragraph{Scope} The project began as a stylized agent-based model of protest, calibrated to a single assumed turnout figure. Answering Q1 and Q2 credibly required more than that model could supply: who actually loses under the reform's legal rules, how a merged pool would reallocate seats, and what past episodes say about the size of shocks. The analysis therefore now links four components (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}): an eligibility accounting under the legal rules; a seat-allocation model built on India's choice rules and published rank lists; a protest model on a district-level population whose parameters are history-matched to event data from four episodes; and a layer of uncertainty analysis that separates what the data identify from what is assumed. The stylized model is kept as a reference specification, so every result can be compared between an assumption-driven and a data-driven version of the same mechanisms.

\paragraph{Contributions} To our knowledge, and after the searches documented in Appendix~\ref{app:search}, this is the first agent-based model of reservation politics and the first study to compare reservation reform designs by the protest they would provoke. Its mechanisms are not new; the claim is narrow. The paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An allocation accounting of the reform.} Eligibility is not the same as seats. Using the over-and-above choice rule that India uses to implement vertical reservation \citep{sonmez2022,aygun2023characterization}, a latent merit model fitted to five years of JEE (Advanced) rank lists and validated against published allotments, and the actual eligibility rules for the OBC creamy layer and the EWS quota, we estimate how an income-only pool would redistribute IIT seats. The reform is not confined to SC and ST families above the line: \EligibilityLose{} of Indians lose eligibility, \EligibilityGain{} gain it, and below-line SC and ST candidates, who keep eligibility, lose most of their seats to below-line General candidates.
\item \textbf{A protest model confronted with event data from several episodes.} A district-level synthetic population (640 Census districts) is history-matched \citep{vernon2010,andrianakis2015} to machine-coded protest event counts (GDELT) for four reservation-related episodes, including a null episode and an upper-caste counter-mobilization, with an audited relevance sample, reported deaths, and two observation-model anchors. Leave-one-episode-out predictions were published, with their hashes, before they were scored.
\item \textbf{A comparison of interventions that separates inputs from findings.} Thirteen scenarios run on paired worlds with bootstrap intervals. Each lever is decomposed into its channels; each lever's effect size gets a prior, from which we report the probability that each lever is strongest; and the comparison is rerun across the assumptions the data leave open, an eleven-variant structural ensemble and a Morris screening.
\item \textbf{A transparent development record}, including two specifications that were badly wrong and how a historical validation test caught them.
\item \textbf{An open, reproducible platform.} Code, derived data and results are public; every number in the paper is generated from result files; a test suite includes an independent reimplementation. The allocation model, the scenario catalogue and the calibration machinery can be reused for other reservation designs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The legal architecture of reservation}
The Constitution permits special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes and for SCs and STs (Articles 15(4) and 16(4)), and reserves legislative seats for SCs and STs (Articles 330 and 332). Central quotas stand at 15\% for SC, 7.5\% for ST and 27\% for OBC \citep{galanter1984,deshpande2013}.

Income tests entered the system through the courts. In \emph{Indra Sawhney v.\ Union of India} (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the 27\% OBC quota introduced after the Mandal Commission, but excluded the ``creamy layer'' of advanced OBC families and capped total reservation at 50\%. The creamy-layer exclusion was reaffirmed for OBC admissions to central educational institutions in \emph{Ashoka Kumar Thakur v.\ Union of India} (2008). The Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, 2019 added a 10\% quota for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) of the General category. The Supreme Court upheld it by 3:2 in \emph{Janhit Abhiyan v.\ Union of India} (2022). To introduce EWS without reducing other categories, the government told the Court it had approved about 2.14 lakh additional seats in central institutions, roughly a 25\% increase in intake \citep{tribune2022}.

The two income tests are not the same test, and neither is a simple cut at \rupee8 lakh of family income.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{OBC creamy layer.} The income-and-wealth test uses a \rupee8 lakh threshold, but income from salaries and from agricultural land is not counted \citep{dopt1993}. Separate status criteria exclude, for example, children of senior (Group A) officers. An OBC family whose gross income exceeds \rupee8 lakh mainly through salary or farm income is therefore non-creamy and eligible today.
\item \textbf{EWS.} Eligibility requires family income from all sources below \rupee8 lakh and excludes families owning 5 acres or more of agricultural land, a residential flat of 1,000 square feet or more, or residential plots above set sizes \citep{dopt2019}. Some General-category families below the income line are therefore ineligible today.
\end{itemize}
SC and ST quotas remain without any income test. In \emph{State of Punjab v.\ Davinder Singh} (2024), a seven-judge bench permitted sub-classification within SC and ST quotas, and several judges suggested that a creamy-layer principle should also apply to them. The Union government stated it would not apply a creamy layer to SCs and STs. Census 2027 will enumerate caste for the first time since 1931 \citep{pib2025}.

\subsection{What an income-only test changes: eligibility}
We take the reform to be one test on gross family income from all sources at \rupee8 lakh, with no category and no asset test. Table~\ref{tab:eligibility} sets out who changes status. Two shares are not observed: the share of above-line OBC families who are non-creamy today (prior 0.3--0.7, central 0.5), and the share of below-line General families who fail the EWS asset tests (prior 0.05--0.25, central 0.15). With the central values, \EligibilityLose{} of the population loses eligibility (range over the priors \EligibilityLoseRange) and \EligibilityGain{} gains it (\EligibilityGainRange). Counting only SC and ST families above the line, which is what treating every income test as the same gross-income cut implies, gives \EligibilityOriginal{}. The difference is the non-creamy OBC families above the line, who are eligible today and would not be.

\subsection{What an income-only test changes: seats}
Eligibility is only half of the material change. Today the reserved categories hold separate quotas. Under the reform the SC, ST, OBC and EWS quotas merge into one pool for all below-line families, so below-line SC and ST candidates compete with below-line General and OBC candidates for the same seats. Whether they gain or lose depends on how the pool is sized and on where each group sits in the merit distribution, which differs substantially by category. Evidence on caste and income as targeting criteria points the same way: caste-based targeting in engineering admissions reaches students poorer than the upper-caste applicants they displace \citep{bertrand2010}, and caste remains a marker of disadvantage among poor households \citep{deshpande2019epw}. Section~\ref{sec:allocation} estimates the seat changes directly.

\subsection{Reservation mobilization in India}
\label{sec:episodes-background}
Reservation has repeatedly produced large mobilizations. In 1990, implementation of the Mandal Commission's OBC quota provoked sustained student protests. In 2006, OBC quotas in central higher education provoked the Youth for Equality movement. Demands for backward-class status drove the Patidar agitation in Gujarat (2015), the Jat agitation in Haryana (2016), the Kapu agitation in Andhra Pradesh (2016), the Gujjar agitation in Rajasthan (2019) and the Maratha Kranti Morcha in Maharashtra, whose largest march, in Mumbai on 9 August 2017, drew estimates of 3 to 5 lakh participants.

Four episodes concern SC and ST protections or the income test directly and are used to calibrate the model (Section~\ref{sec:episodes}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{2 April 2018.} In \emph{Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v.\ State of Maharashtra} (March 2018), the Supreme Court diluted arrest provisions of the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act. No one lost a quota. A Bharat Bandh followed on 2 April; 11 to 14 people died. It was organized with little central leadership or party backing: the first call came from a local leader in Punjab and spread through WhatsApp and local Dalit organizations \citep{scroll2018whatsapp}. Same-day reports spoke of ``thousands'' of protesters \citep{aljazeera2018news}; later commentary of ``hundreds of thousands'' \citep{aljazeera2018opinion}. Parliament restored the provisions later that year.
\item \textbf{6 September 2018.} Upper-caste organizations called a Bharat Bandh against the restoring amendment. It was strongest in Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, with no deaths reported.
\item \textbf{21 August 2024.} After \emph{Davinder Singh}, a Bharat Bandh demanded that no creamy layer or sub-classification apply to SC and ST quotas. Several opposition parties backed it, yet it drew a mixed response, and SC and ST communities that expected to gain from sub-classification opposed it.
\item \textbf{January 2019.} The EWS amendment passed with little street protest, which makes it a useful null episode.
\end{itemize}

From these episodes we draw stylized facts that the model should reproduce or respect:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{S\arabic*.}]
\item A purely symbolic threat to SC/ST protections can produce national, bandh-scale mobilization (2018).
\item Mobilization is organized around bandh calls and concentrated on those days.
\item Large mobilizations do not require party backing (2018 was locally organized), and party backing does not guarantee them (2024). Party backing is at most one amplifier among several.
\item Groups split when a reform creates winners inside them (2024).
\item Deaths occur at some large mobilizations and not others, and states often concede afterwards (2018).
\end{enumerate}
S3 matters for the design of the model. It would be natural to treat opposition-party backing as the engine of large mobilizations; the 2018 record does not support that, and the calibration below treats party backing as an episode-specific input.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reservation design} The closest formal work is in matching theory. \citet{sonmez2022} formalize how India implements vertical and horizontal reservations, and \citet{aygun2020,aygun2023deres} study the de-reservation of unfilled OBC seats in IIT admissions; \citet{aygun2023characterization} characterize the over-and-above rule axiomatically. We use their choice rules rather than inventing an allocation mechanism. Empirical work compares caste and income as targeting criteria \citep{bertrand2010,deshpande2019epw,deshpande2019wd}. An incentivized experiment finds that SC and ST beneficiaries are judged less competent even when affirmative action is income-based \citep{deshpande2026merit}, which bears on whether an income test would remove the symbolic charge of reservation. None of this work models the political response to reform.

\paragraph{Protest models} Threshold models \citep{granovetter1978,schelling1978}, critical-mass theory \citep{marwell1993} and preference falsification \citep{kuran1991} explain why collective action can be all-or-nothing and hard to predict. \citet{epstein2002} began a line of agent-based models of civil violence; \citet{srbljinovic2003} model ethnic mobilization, and \citet{lemos2018} compares model output with the size, duration and recurrence of events in the Social Conflict Analysis Database. \citet{thron2015} caution that such models may display generic self-organized criticality rather than the mechanisms their authors intend. That critique applies here, and it is one reason we test mechanisms against several episodes, report which results are inputs, and use a structural ensemble. On India specifically, \citet{wilkinson2004} shows that electoral incentives shape whether states prevent or permit communal violence, which motivates the concession and policing mechanisms.

\paragraph{Validating agent-based models} \citet{windrum2007} survey empirical validation of agent-based models. We follow the history-matching approach of \citet{vernon2010} and \citet{andrianakis2015}, which rules out parameter sets inconsistent with data rather than fitting a single best set, and which suits a stochastic model with several unidentified shock sizes. Simulation-based inference \citep{cranmer2020} is an alternative that we leave for future work. For global sensitivity analysis we use Morris screening \citep{morris1991,campolongo2007,saltelli2008}. The model description follows the spirit of the ODD protocol \citep{grimm2020}.

\paragraph{Event data} GDELT \citep{leetaru2013} codes events automatically from news reports. It over-reports events in capitals and media centres \citep{hammond2014}, which our observation model allows for. ACLED \citep{raleigh2010} covers India from 2016 and would be a natural cross-check; we did not use it here.

\section{Theory and hypotheses}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Loss aversion and the politics of retrenchment}
Prospect theory holds that losses loom larger than equivalent gains, with a loss-aversion coefficient near 2.25 \citep{kahneman1979,tversky1992}. \citet{pierson1994,pierson1996}, building on \citet{weaver1986}, argues that retrenchment is harder than expansion because costs are concentrated, immediate and traceable while benefits are diffuse. Governments that retrench successfully rely on blame-avoidance strategies: \emph{obfuscation}, which lowers the visibility of cuts; \emph{division}, including grandfathering, which spares current beneficiaries; \emph{compensation} for losers; and \emph{blame-sharing} through broad coalitions. We model the transparent versions of these strategies. Obfuscation is excluded on ethical grounds (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\subsection{Group identity and symbolic threat}
Social identity theory holds that part of individuals' self-concept derives from group membership, and that perceived threats to a group's status provoke collective responses \citep{tajfel1979}. For SC and ST communities, caste-based reservation is a constitutional acknowledgement of historical injustice as well as a material benefit. Removing caste as the criterion is therefore a symbolic threat independent of any individual's eligibility. Stylized fact S1 is direct evidence that this channel operates at national scale; the 2018 bandh followed a ruling that removed no one's quota.

\subsection{Thresholds, cascades and organization}
In threshold models, each individual joins once the share of others participating exceeds a personal threshold \citep{granovetter1978,schelling1978}. Aggregate outcomes can then be highly sensitive to the distribution of thresholds, and cascades can be global or fizzle depending on small changes \citep{watts2002}. Critical-mass theory stresses that a small, highly motivated core can make collective action viable for everyone else \citep{marwell1993}, and preference falsification means that latent support can be invisible until a cascade begins \citep{kuran1991}. Resource-mobilization and political-process theories stress that organizations and political opportunities convert latent grievance into action \citep{mccarthy1977,mcadam1982,tarrow1994}.

\subsection{Repression and backfire}
The relationship between repression and dissent is contested: repression sometimes deters and sometimes escalates \citep{lichbach1987,davenport2007}. \citet{hess2006} describe conditions under which repression backfires by generating outrage. For India, \citet{rydzak2019} finds that internet shutdowns are associated with increases in violent collective action rather than decreases, and \citet{rydzak2020} find little evidence that shutdowns quell protest elsewhere. Because the literature supports both deterrence and backfire, the model does not assume either: the response of turnout to deaths is a parameter whose prior runs from deterrence to strong backfire.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*.}]
\item Grandfathering and a glide path reduce protest by deferring material loss (loss aversion).
\item Expanding seats reduces protest, following the EWS 2019 precedent.
\item Retaining caste categories with an income filter inside them reduces protest by reducing symbolic threat.
\item Sub-classification favouring the most-deprived reduces protest by splitting the coalition (S4).
\item A data-first commission with cross-party consensus reduces protest by reducing organization and symbolic threat.
\item Compensating direct losers reduces protest (Pierson's compensation strategy).
\item Credible statutory guarantees reduce protest by limiting feared future changes.
\item Internet shutdowns reduce protest.
\item Heavy policing reduces protest.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The allocation model: who gains and loses seats}
\label{sec:allocation}

\subsection{Choice rules}
India implements vertical reservation with the \emph{over-and-above} rule \citep{sonmez2022,aygun2023characterization}. Open-category seats go first to the highest-merit applicants of any category; each reserved category's seats then go to its highest-merit remaining members. Reserved-category candidates who would win an open seat on merit therefore do not use up a reserved seat. We apply the same rule to the reform, with the open share unchanged at 40.5\% and one reserve category, ``family income below \rupee8 lakh'', holding the rest (59.5\% in the main case; 49.5\% and 69.5\% as variants).

Actual IIT admission is a centralized multi-programme assignment in which top reserved-category candidates often take reserved seats at preferred programmes. For the aggregate number of seats by category, this makes the outcome closer to a \emph{reserved-first} order, in which reserved seats are filled before open seats. We check both orders against the actual category-wise allotments (Section~\ref{sec:allocation-validation}) and use the one that reproduces them as the main case. Horizontal reservations and candidates' preferences over programmes are not modelled.

\subsection{Latent merit}
The JEE (Advanced) Common Rank List records each qualified candidate's rank and category. We transcribed the lists for 2019--2025 (Appendix~\ref{app:data}) and model each category's candidates as normally distributed on a common latent merit scale, with the General category fixed at mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The number of candidates per category comes from the official reports. Given the parameters, rank $r$ sits at the point $z$ where the expected number of candidates above $z$ equals $r-\tfrac12$, and the probability that the candidate at that rank belongs to category $c$ is proportional to $N_c\,\phi_c(z)$. The means and standard deviations maximize the likelihood of the observed categories at every rank. The 2019 and 2020 lists failed an internal consistency check and are excluded; the fits use \AllocYears.

\subsection{Family income}
Family income is not observed per candidate. Each category's share of candidates above the line is drawn from a prior: General 0.70--0.95 (the category includes OBC creamy-layer candidates and General candidates above the EWS line), OBC-NCL 0.10--0.40 (above the line on gross income but non-creamy), SC and ST 0.10--0.45; GEN-EWS candidates are below by definition. A merit gradient (0--0.8 on the logit scale per standard deviation of merit) lets richer candidates sit higher in each category's distribution. A draw is kept only if the implied share of admitted IIT students above the line is 28--45\%, the range consistent with the parental-income tables of the JEE reports once income growth is allowed for. We make \AllocDraws{} draws per year and keep \AllocDrawsKept{} in total.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:allocation-validation}
In sample, the fitted category shares match the observed shares by rank decile to within \AllocMaxDecileError{} percentage points. Out of sample, the GEN-EWS and OBC-NCL rank lists end at the same aggregate-marks cutoff, as do the SC and ST lists, so the latent cutoffs implied by each list's length should agree within each pair. They agree to within \AllocGapEWSOBC{} standard deviations for GEN-EWS and OBC-NCL, and to within \AllocGapSCSTOthers{} for SC and ST in every year except \AllocGapSCSTWorstYear{} (\AllocGapSCSTWorst). Under the reserved-first order, the model's status-quo seat counts by category match the actual IIT allotments to within \AllocMaxAllotmentGap{} seats per category and year; the open-first order misses by up to \AllocMaxAllotmentGapOpenFirst{} seats. The reserved-first order is therefore the main case. NEET, where the same approach would cover medical admissions, is excluded because the fitted tails failed a check against the top-ranked candidates.

\section{The protest model}
\label{sec:model}

The model is summarized in the ODD format \citep{grimm2020} in Appendix~\ref{app:odd}; this section gives the details. Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows what drives one agent's decision each day. The model has two specifications. The \emph{stylized} specification is the original model, retained as a reference and for comparison with earlier results. The \emph{grounded} specification replaces its assumed inputs wherever data allow; Table~\ref{tab:specifications} lists the differences.

\subsection{Population}
The grounded population has $N=120{,}000$ agents, each representing about 12{,}200 people of a population of 146 crore. Agents are allocated to the 640 Census 2011 districts in proportion to population. Within each district the SC and ST shares are the district's own (Census 2011); the rest are split between OBC and General with the state's ratio of OBC to OBC-plus-other household heads in NFHS-5. National shares are then SC 16.7\%, ST 8.6\%, OBC 45.7\% and General 29.0\%. SC and ST agents are assigned with equal probability to a better-off tier or a most-deprived tier. The probability of being above the \rupee8 lakh line is 30\% for General and 17\% for OBC; for SC it is 19\% in the better-off tier and 5\% in the most-deprived tier, and for ST 16\% and 4\%. These shares order groups consistently with survey evidence on the caste gradient of income and wealth \citep{bharti2018,price2023}. Above-line OBC agents are creamy layer with probability 0.5, and below-line General agents fail the EWS asset tests with probability 0.15.

Each agent draws an identity strength $\iota_i\sim\mathrm{Beta}(2,3)$ and a standard-normal threshold score $z_i$. Neighbourhoods of about 100 agents are formed within state and group blocks ordered by district, so a neighbourhood spans about three districts. A share $h$ of agents live in single-group neighbourhoods and the rest in mixed neighbourhoods whose composition matches the national mix; $h$ is estimated in calibration. The stylized population has the same structure without districts, with $h=1$ (complete segregation), NFHS-4 group shares (OBC 42\%, General 32.8\%), and no distinction between the creamy layer and the income line.

\subsection{Grievance}
Agent $i$ in eligibility segment $k$ has a material change $m_k$ (positive for a loss). In the grounded specification $m_k$ is the relative material change of Table~\ref{tab:allocation}, which puts the loss of an above-line SC candidate at 1. In the stylized specification it is 1 for SC and ST above the line, 0.35 for SC and ST below, 0.25 for OBC below, $-0.15$ for General below, and 0 otherwise. Losses are weighted by loss aversion, $L(m)=\lambda m$ for $m>0$ and $m$ otherwise. Symbolic threat $s_g$ is 1.0 for SC, 0.9 for ST, 0.45 for OBC and $-0.4$ for General, times 0.8 for the most-deprived tier; in the grounded specification it is scaled by the 2018 episode's estimated magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:episodes}). Grievance on day $t$ is
\begin{equation}
G_{i,t}=w_m\,L(m_{k(i)})+w_s\,\iota_i\left(\tilde s_i+\mu_{g,t}\,\mathbb{1}[\tilde s_i>0]\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde s_i$ is the agent's tier-adjusted symbolic threat and $\mu_{g,t}$ the accumulated martyr effect for the group.

\subsection{Social influence, organization and decision}
Let $n_{c,t}$ be the share of neighbourhood $c$ protesting on day $t$ and $v_{g,t}$ the national share of group $g$. Social pull saturates:
\begin{equation}
S_{i,t}=\beta_n\tanh\!\left(\frac{n_{c(i),t-1}}{n^*}\right)+\beta_v\tanh\!\left(\frac{v_{g,t-1}}{v^*}\right).
\end{equation}
Organization contributes $a\,o_g\,b_t$, where $o_g$ is group organizational capacity (SC 0.6, ST 0.4, OBC 0.4, General 0.1), $a$ the opposition-party amplifier and $b_t$ the mobilization level: 1.0 on bandh days and 0.15 otherwise. The agent protests with probability
\begin{equation}
P_{i,t}=\left[1+\exp\!\left(-\frac{G_{i,t}+S_{i,t}+a\,o_g\,b_t-c_t-\theta_{i,t}}{\tau}\right)\right]^{-1},
\qquad \theta_{i,0}=\bar\theta+\sigma_\theta z_i ,
\end{equation}
where $c_t$ is a turnout cost imposed by heavy policing on bandh days. Each day of protest raises an agent's threshold by the fatigue increment $f$.

\subsection{Bandh calls, concession and backlash}
In the stylized specification bandhs are called on fixed days (3, 13 and 26). In the grounded specification, organizations call a bandh for the next day once the turnout that a bandh would draw, given today's grievance and social pull, reaches 10 lakh. The first call can take effect on day 3; after each bandh there is a refractory period of 10--13 days, and there are at most four calls. The government concedes with a daily hazard that rises logistically with pressure, defined as the largest daily turnout so far in crore plus deaths so far divided by 20 (midpoint 1, width 0.25), up to 15\% a day. A concession cuts material loss and the threatened groups' symbolic threat by 60\%, cancels any scheduled bandh, and gives the General category a symbolic threat of 0.3, which can produce a counter-mobilization.

\subsection{Deaths and the martyr effect}
In the stylized specification, deaths on day $t$ are Poisson with mean $\kappa$ times the day's protester-days in crore, and every death raises $\mu_g$ by $\delta=0.012$ for each group in proportion to its share of protesters, up to 0.35. In the grounded specification deaths are negative binomial with dispersion estimated in calibration, the rate on bandh days is ten times the ordinary-day rate, and deaths are split into police-attributed deaths (half at baseline) and others. A response parameter $\rho$ multiplies the martyr effect: $\rho=1$ is the reference backfire, $\rho=0$ no response, and $\rho<0$ deterrence. Heavy policing raises only police-attributed deaths.

\subsection{Uncertainty and paired sampling}
Each Monte Carlo run draws a plausible world. In the stylized specification this means uniform multipliers on $\lambda$ (0.7--1.3), symbolic threat (0.8--1.2), $a$ (0.85--1.15), $\beta_n$ and $\beta_v$ (0.8--1.2 each) and $\kappa$ (0.5--2.0), plus $\mathcal N(0,0.1)$ on $\bar\theta$. In the grounded specification, the parameters informed by the episodes ($\bar\theta$, $\sigma_\theta$, the reform's symbolic threat, the bandh-day death rate and dispersion) are instead taken from one parameter set drawn at random from those that survive calibration, and the reform's symbolic threat is additionally multiplied by 0.8--1.2. Run $r$ uses world seed $1000+r$ and campaign seed $5000+r$ for every scenario, so scenario comparisons are paired. Effects are reported as the median over runs of the paired ratio (scenario over baseline in the same world), with 95\% percentile-bootstrap intervals over runs \citep{efron1993}, and as the share of runs in which the scenario exceeds the baseline.

\textbf{One campaign.} Build the population; draw a world; apply the scenario's interventions. Then for $t=0,\dots,39$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=14pt]
\item compute $G_{i,t}$, $S_{i,t}$ and $P_{i,t}$ for every agent;
\item draw each agent's protest decision and raise the thresholds of those who protest;
\item update neighbourhood and national turnout; decide whether a bandh is called for tomorrow and whether the government concedes;
\item draw deaths and update the martyr effect.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Episode data and calibration}
\label{sec:episodes}

\subsection{Event counts}
Protest turnout in India is rarely counted, and the estimates that exist differ by an order of magnitude (for 2018, from ``thousands'' to ``hundreds of thousands''). Instead of calibrating to a headcount, we calibrate to the number of protest events reported. From the GDELT 1.0 daily event files we take every event with CAMEO root code 14 (protest) located in India on each episode's core days, and keep those whose source article URL matches episode-specific keywords (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). Keyword filters admit irrelevant events: a fuel-price bandh on 10 September 2018, unrelated state bandhs in August 2024, and a general strike during the EWS amendment. For each episode a sample of matched URLs was labelled as relevant or not from the article's URL text; the posterior precision on core days, Beta$(1+\text{relevant},\,1+\text{irrelevant})$, multiplies the count. To absorb GDELT's changing coverage, counts are expressed per 1,000 GDELT events located in India on the same days. Table~\ref{tab:episodes} lists the episodes and targets.

\subsection{Simulating an episode}
Each mechanism episode is simulated as a two-day campaign, an announcement day and a bandh day, with no material change. Its shock is a symbolic threat vector (which groups feel threatened, and in which direction) scaled by an unknown magnitude. For 2018 the vector is (1, 0.9, 0, $-$0.2) for SC, ST, OBC and General; for the September 2018 upper-caste bandh ($-$0.3, $-$0.3, 0.3, 1); for 2024 (1, 0.9, 0, 0) with the most-deprived tier's share of the threat estimated, since many of them stood to gain from sub-classification; and for the EWS amendment the reform's own vector. Party backing is coded from reporting: 0.2 for 2018 (locally organized), 0.1 for September 2018, 0.6 for 2024 and 0 for 2019. It sets the party amplifier, with backing 0.6 corresponding to the reform scenario's $a=1.5$. The magnitudes are relative to the reform's symbolic threat to SC agents, so a 2018 magnitude of 1 means that the reform threatens caste recognition as much as the 2018 ruling did.

\subsection{Observation model}
Expected reported events are $\alpha\sum_s (P_s/10^5)^{\gamma}$, where $P_s$ is protester-days in state $s$, with Delhi's term multiplied by a media factor, since national media concentrate there \citep{hammond2014}. The concave form means that a bandh spread over many states produces more reported events than a single rally of the same total size. The scale $\alpha$, exponent $\gamma$ and Delhi factor are unknown. They are tied down by two anchor episodes with published crowd estimates: the Maratha march in Mumbai on 9 August 2017 (1 to 20 lakh person-days) and the Patidar agitation of August 2015 (3 to 20 lakh).

\subsection{History matching}
Fifteen unknowns get uniform priors: $\bar\theta$ (5--8.5), $\sigma_\theta$ (0.8--2.2), $h$ (0.5--1), the four episode magnitudes, the 2024 deprived-tier share, the bandh-day death rate ($10^{0.3}$ to $10^{3}$ per crore protester-days), its dispersion, the three observation parameters and the two anchor crowd sizes. For each parameter set, every mechanism episode is simulated with two seeds. A set is \emph{implausible} if, for any target, the standardized distance
\begin{equation}
I=\frac{|\log \hat y-\log y|}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\text{obs}}+\sigma^2_{\text{Poisson}}+\sigma^2_{\text{sim}}+\sigma^2_{\text{disc}}}}
\end{equation}
exceeds 3 \citep{vernon2010,andrianakis2015}, where the variances are observation error (from the precision interval), Poisson counting error, simulation noise between seeds, and a model discrepancy of 0.5 on the log scale. Deaths on the bandh day are compared on the natural scale with negative-binomial variance and a discrepancy of 2 deaths. Three waves of \CalibWaveSize{} sets are run; each later wave samples within the range of the previous wave's survivors. The sets that survive all waves form the not-ruled-out-yet (NROY) set used by the grounded specification.

\subsection{Out-of-sample tests}
Two kinds of test use data not used to fit.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Leave one episode out.} For each of the three bandh episodes, a separate history match uses only the other episodes. Its survivors predict the held-out episode's bandh-day deaths and its distribution of protest across states; the held-out magnitude is not identified without that episode's data, so the prediction integrates over its prior. The predictions are written to files and hashed (SHA-256) before a separate script compares them with the observations.
\item \textbf{Spatial distribution.} Event counts by state are not used in calibration. For each episode, the Spearman correlation across large states between the model's protester-days and the observed event counts is compared with two naive baselines: state population and state SC-plus-ST population.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Model development: what went wrong and how it was caught}
\label{sec:development}

The stylized model is the fourth specification. We report the earlier ones because the failures show which mechanisms matter and why validation must go beyond calibration. Every number in this section comes from \path{experiments/replay_model_development.py}, which re-runs each earlier specification with the current code.

\paragraph{Iteration 1: unbounded contagion} The first specification used linear social influence, $S_{i,t}=1.6\,n_{c,t-1}+25\,v_{g,t-1}$, narrow thresholds ($\sigma_\theta=0.55$, $\tau=0.22$) and equal weight on material loss and symbolic threat ($w_m=1.0$, $w_s=2.2$). At every mean threshold tried (2.8 to 3.3), it placed about \HistOnePeak{} people on the street on the peak day, essentially every SC, ST and OBC agent. Even without contagion, the first bandh day would have drawn \HistOneBandhSC{} of SC agents, against \HistFinalBandhSC{} in the final model; and linear contagion has no ceiling, so once a group's national turnout reached a few percent the visibility term alone exceeded any threshold. The fix was to bound social influence with a saturating function.

\paragraph{Iteration 2: saturating contagion, narrow thresholds} With saturating influence the model could be calibrated: at $\bar\theta=5.1$ it gave a baseline peak of \HistTwoBaseline{}. It had a knife-edge: turnout fell \HistTwoFallFactor-fold between $\bar\theta=4.9$ (\HistTwoPeakAtFourNine) and $5.0$ (\HistTwoPeakAtFive), so any intervention that nudged grievance down appeared to eliminate protest. Grandfathering changed the peak by \HistTwoChangeGrandfathering{} and compensation by \HistTwoChangeCompensation. The decisive evidence came from a validation test: removing all material loss, the analogue of the 2018 ruling, left \HistTwoSymbolicOnly{} protesters, \HistTwoSymbolicShare{} of the baseline. That contradicts S1. Iteration 2 had been calibrated to a plausible turnout, but for the wrong reason.

\paragraph{Iteration 3: reweighting alone does not fix it} Raising $w_s$ and lowering $w_m$, recalibrating $\bar\theta$ each time, left the symbolic-only shock below 5\% of the baseline however the weights were set (Table~\ref{tab:iteration3}). With a narrow threshold distribution, removing any component of grievance drops the population below the tipping point together.

Threshold heterogeneity is a substantive assumption; it determines whether interventions look marginal or miraculous. The same lessons motivated the grounded specification: a single turnout target and a single validation episode are too weak a test, so the grounded model is confronted with several episodes, including a null episode and one in which the upper castes, not SCs and STs, mobilized.

\section{Calibration results}
\label{sec:calibration-results}

\paragraph{What survives} Of \CalibWaveSize{} parameter sets drawn from the priors, \CalibFirstWaveKept{} were not ruled out by the event counts, deaths and anchors. After \CalibWaves{} waves, \CalibKept{} sets survive (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}). By construction, every survivor reproduces every target within the stated uncertainty, including the September 2018 upper-caste bandh, which the model generates from the same mechanisms with the threat pointing the other way. The fit to the EWS null episode is the loosest: the survivors' median event rate lies above the observed one, and the fit relies on the wide observation interval that the low precision of the EWS filter produces.

\paragraph{What the data identify} The mean threshold is \CalibTheta{} (90\% interval \CalibThetaInterval), close to the stylized model's 6.6, which was tuned to an independent turnout target. The 2018 shock is \CalibMagnitudeTwentyEighteen{} (\CalibMagnitudeTwentyEighteenInterval) times the stylized reform's symbolic threat. Relative to 2018, the 2024 shock has median \CalibRatioTwentyFour{} (\CalibRatioTwentyFourInterval), the September 2018 upper-caste shock \CalibRatioUpperCaste{} (\CalibRatioUpperCasteInterval), and the EWS amendment \CalibRatioEWS{} (\CalibRatioEWSInterval). The medians order the episodes as reporting does, but every interval is wide. The share of single-group neighbourhoods is \CalibMixing{} (\CalibMixingInterval): the data do not support complete segregation, but do not pin mixing down either.

\paragraph{What they do not} The implied turnout on 2 April 2018 is \CalibTurnoutTwentyEighteen{} lakh, with a 90\% interval of \CalibTurnoutTwentyEighteenInterval{} lakh. Event counts trade off against the observation model's scale and exponent, and the two anchors narrow that trade-off only partly. The event data rule out the lowest reading of the press reports (``thousands''), but they cannot distinguish a few lakh from a few crore. This is the central limitation of calibrating to event data here: it constrains relative magnitudes far better than absolute turnout. The bandh-day death rate is \CalibBandhDeathRate{} per crore protester-days (\CalibBandhDeathRateInterval), far above the stylized model's ordinary-day rate of 4, because 11 to 14 people died on a single day in 2018.

\paragraph{Out-of-sample tests} The leave-one-episode-out predictions were committed to the public repository, with their hashes, before they were scored (Table~\ref{tab:loeo}). They are weak.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deaths.} Without its own data, 2018's bandh-day deaths are predicted at a median of \LoeoTwentyEighteenDeaths{} against 11--14 observed, but with an interval so wide as to be uninformative. For the two peaceful bandhs the model predicts deaths where none occurred. The model has one bandh-day death rate for all episodes, and the data say that violence is specific to the episode: it depends on policing, local counter-mobilization and chance in ways the model does not represent. The death outputs of the reform scenarios should be read in that light.
\item \textbf{Geography.} Across \LoeoStates{} large states, the model's distribution of protest correlates with the observed distribution of events at \LoeoTwentyEighteenSpatialModel{} for 2018 when that episode is held out, better than state population (\LoeoTwentyEighteenSpatialPopulation) and state SC-plus-ST population (\LoeoTwentyEighteenSpatialSCST). For 2024 it roughly ties the SC-plus-ST baseline (\LoeoTwentyTwentyFourSpatialModel{} against \LoeoTwentyTwentyFourSpatialSCST); for the upper-caste bandh it does worse than both baselines (\LoeoUpperCasteSpatialModel{} against \LoeoUpperCasteSpatialPopulation{} and \LoeoUpperCasteSpatialSCST). Table~\ref{tab:spatial} gives the same comparison with the full calibration. The district geography adds little predictive power beyond demography.
\end{itemize}
These results limit what the grounded specification can claim. It is grounded in the sense that its inputs come from data and its survivors reproduce four episodes' event counts; it is not a validated forecasting model of where, how large or how deadly protest will be.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The abrupt switch}
In the grounded specification an abrupt switch produces a median peak of \PeakGroundedBaseline{} (80\% interval \PeakRangeGroundedBaseline{} lakh), \CumulativeGroundedBaseline{} participants and a median of \DeathsGroundedBaseline{} deaths. Organizations call the first bandh on day \GroundedFirstBandhDay{} in the median run, and the government concedes in \GroundedConcededShare{} of runs, after a median of \GroundedConcessionDay{} days. The stylized specification, tuned to a peak of 20 lakh to 1 crore, gives \PeakCentralBaseline{}.

The grounded level should not be read as a forecast. It is \CalibMagnitudeTwentyEighteen{} times the stylized symbolic threat because that is what the 2018 episode implies, it inherits the wide range of 2018 turnouts that the event data allow, and it assumes that the reform shocks caste recognition as much as the 2018 ruling did ($R=1$) and that opposition parties back the protest. Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} shows that the level moves by more than an order of magnitude across these assumptions. The comparisons between interventions, which are paired within each world, are the object of the analysis.

\paragraph{Where the grievance comes from} Symbolic threat is \SymbolicShareOfGrievance{} of all grievance that pushes agents towards protest (Figure~\ref{fig:grievance}). Removing all material change cuts the peak by \GChanNoMaterial; removing all symbolic threat cuts it by \GChanNoSymbolic. Symbolic threat is the larger driver, but the material loss is not marginal.

\paragraph{Who protests and where} SC agents account for \GroupShareSC{} of protester-days, ST agents for \GroupShareST, OBC agents for \GroupShareOBC{} and General agents, who counter-mobilize after a concession, for \GroupShareGeneral. Among SC and ST agents, \TierBaselineBetterOff{} of the better-off tier and \TierBaselineDeprived{} of the most-deprived tier protest at least once. It does not reproduce where the 2018 bandh was reported: Punjab, where the 2018 call began, had a far larger share of events than the model gives it, and West Bengal and Tamil Nadu far smaller ones. Local organization, which the model does not represent, shapes where a bandh takes hold; this is consistent with the weak spatial tests of Section~\ref{sec:calibration-results}.

\paragraph{Keeping caste as the qualifier dominates} Keeping caste quotas and adding an income filter inside them (L3) changes the grounded peak by \PeakChangeGroundedHybridCasteSubquotas{} (95\% CI \PeakChangeCIGroundedHybridCasteSubquotas), and the stylized peak by \PeakChangeCentralHybridCasteSubquotas. In the grounded specification the lever does two things: it lowers symbolic threat, and, according to the allocation model, it turns the reform's large seat loss for below-line SC and ST candidates into a gain (Section~\ref{sec:allocation}). This is also the lever that least resembles the reform: it keeps caste as a criterion, so it changes the question more than it answers it.

\paragraph{Among levers that keep the income-only end state, process and phasing come first} A data-first commission with cross-party consensus (L5) changes the peak by \PeakChangeGroundedConsensusCommission{} (\PeakChangeCIGroundedConsensusCommission), and grandfathering with a ten-year glide (L1) by \PeakChangeGroundedGrandfathering{} (\PeakChangeCIGroundedGrandfathering). Their order is close in the grounded specification, and Section~\ref{sec:mapping} shows that it depends on the priors over how strongly each lever acts. Statutory guarantees (L7, \PeakChangeGroundedCredibleGuarantees) and sub-classification (L4, \PeakChangeGroundedSubClassification) follow.

\paragraph{Seat expansion and compensation do little} Expanding seats by a quarter (L2) changes the grounded peak by only \PeakChangeGroundedSeatExpansion{}, against \PeakChangeCentralSeatExpansion{} in the stylized specification. The difference is the allocation model: the stylized specification assumed that expansion removes the below-line loss, while the allocation model shows that a merged pool with a quarter more seats still takes most of their seats from below-line SC and ST candidates (\AllocExpandedSCBelow{} for SC). The EWS precedent does not transfer, because EWS added a category without merging any. Compensating above-line losers (L6) is weakest (\PeakChangeGroundedCompensation), because it reaches a small minority and does not touch symbolic threat.

\paragraph{Packages} The managed transition (C1) keeps the income-only end state and changes the peak by \PeakChangeGroundedManagedTransition{} (\PeakChangeCIGroundedManagedTransition); the hybrid package (C2) by \PeakChangeGroundedHybridPackage.

\paragraph{Material grievance matters more than the stylized model assumed} Removing all material change (V) changes the grounded peak by \PeakChangeGroundedSymbolicOnlyValidation{}, against \PeakChangeCentralSymbolicOnlyValidation{} in the stylized specification. The allocation model makes the material loss larger and broader than the stylized specification assumed, because below-line SC and ST candidates lose most of their seats, not a small share. Symbolic threat is still the larger driver, but not by as much.

\subsection{Suppression}
Internet shutdowns (B1) change the grounded peak by \PeakChangeGroundedInternetShutdown{} and multiply deaths by \DeathRatioGroundedInternetShutdown{}; heavy policing (B2) changes it by \PeakChangeGroundedHeavyPolicing{} (\PeakChangeCIGroundedHeavyPolicing) and multiplies deaths by \DeathRatioGroundedHeavyPolicing. Part of each death ratio is an input: B1 assumes 2.5 times as many violent deaths and B2 three times as many police-attributed deaths. Section~\ref{sec:inputs} separates the two. Unlike the stylized specification, where heavy policing raises cumulative participation by \CumulativeChangeCentralHeavyPolicing, the grounded specification shows a change of \CumulativeChangeGroundedHeavyPolicing: campaigns end in concession within days, so the martyr effect has little time to act. Whether suppression backfires on participation is therefore not a robust result; that it costs lives is, since it follows from the assumed rise in deaths.

\subsection{What is input and what is emergent}
\label{sec:inputs}
Every lever changes more than one input. Re-running each lever with one channel at a time, on the same paired worlds, separates them (Table~\ref{tab:channels}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{L3} works through both of its channels. Its symbolic channel alone changes the peak by \GChanLThreeSymbolic{} and its allocation channel alone, which turns below-line seat losses into gains, by \GChanLThreeAllocation.
\item \textbf{L5}'s effect comes mostly from its assumed 20\% cut in symbolic threat (\GChanLFiveSymbolic); withdrawing party machinery alone gives \GChanLFiveAmplifier. Given S3, that is the more defensible channel, but it is also the less certain one.
\item \textbf{L1} works almost entirely through material loss (\GChanLOneMaterial; its 5\% symbolic cut gives \GChanLOneSymbolic).
\item \textbf{B2.} The turnout cost alone changes the peak by \GChanBTwoTurnoutCost; the extra deaths and martyr response alone change it by \GChanBTwoViolence{} and deaths by \GChanBTwoViolenceDeaths. The rise in deaths under heavy policing is almost all input: the scenario assumes three times as many police-attributed deaths. \textbf{B1} without its assumed extra violence changes the peak by \GChanBOneCoordination{} and deaths by \GChanBOneCoordinationDeaths; with it, deaths change by \GChanBOneFullDeaths.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Material versus symbolic weight}
\label{sec:breakeven}
The ratio of the symbolic to the material grievance weight is not identified by any turnout target, and the episodes involve no material change, so they do not identify $w_m$ either. In the stylized specification, a sweep that holds $w_s w_m$ fixed and recalibrates $\bar\theta$ at each point finds that a material lever is strongest only when $w_s/w_m$ is at most \BreakEvenStylizedMaterialStrongestBelow, against the reference \BreakEvenStylizedReferenceRatio; the 2018-type validation check admits ratios \BreakEvenStylizedAdmitted. In the grounded specification, where $\bar\theta$ comes from the episodes, we multiply $w_m$ directly (Table~\ref{tab:materialsweep}). No material lever becomes the strongest at any multiplier up to \MaterialSweepMaxMultiplier. Grandfathering overtakes the commission once $w_m$ is doubled, and compensation stops being the weakest lever once $w_m$ is quadrupled; grandfathering approaches L3 only at the largest multiplier, where the baseline peak is implausibly large. A heavier material weight therefore reorders the middle of the ranking, not its top.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence changed} Comparing the grounded specification with the stylized one shows which conclusions depended on assumptions. Three did. First, the reform is not a small eligibility change confined to SC and ST families above the line: under the actual creamy-layer and EWS rules, about \EligibilityLose{} of Indians lose eligibility, and in IIT admissions below-line SC and ST candidates, who keep eligibility, lose most of their seats to below-line General candidates. Second, seat expansion on the EWS model does little, because a merged pool still ranks SC and ST candidates against General candidates; the stylized specification had assumed that expansion removes the below-line loss. Third, that heavy policing raises total participation depends on an assumed response of turnout to deaths, and does not hold in the grounded specification, where campaigns end in concession quickly. Material grievance also matters more than the stylized specification assumed, because the seat losses of below-line SC and ST candidates are large.

\paragraph{What survived} The ordering at the top and bottom of the lever ranking survived every analysis in this paper: keeping caste as the qualifier with an income filter inside it (L3) cuts protest most, and compensating the direct losers (L6) is among the least effective, joined in the grounded specification by seat expansion (L2). L3 is first in both specifications, across the priors over effect sizes, across the structural variants, across the Morris design, and across the grounded specification's unidentified shock size, party backing and material weight. Compensation is last or second to last in almost all of them; the exception is a material weight at least four times its reference value, which implies turnouts far beyond any recorded protest. The reason is structural. Most of the mobilizable grievance is symbolic and spread across the SC and ST population, and in the grounded specification the material loss is also concentrated on below-line SC and ST candidates; L3 removes much of both, while compensation reaches a small minority and touches neither.

\paragraph{What did not settle} Among levers that keep the income-only end state, the order of the data-first commission (L5) and grandfathering (L1) is not settled. At the reference values the commission is ahead; averaged over the priors on how strongly each lever acts, grandfathering is ahead more often (the commission wins with probability \MapGroundedLFiveBeatsLOne). The order depends on how strongly a commission would lower symbolic threat, on the size of the reform's shock (grandfathering comes first when $R=0.5$), and on the material weight (grandfathering comes first once $w_m$ is doubled), and none of these is identified. The decomposition shows that most of L5's effect in the model comes from its assumed cut in symbolic threat. Given that the 2018 bandh was large without party backing, the claim that consensus works by withholding party machinery is the weaker half of the mechanism. A commission's value, if it has one, would lie in changing what the reform means to those it affects.

\paragraph{Compensation and the retrenchment literature} \citet{pierson1994} treats compensation as a blame-avoidance tool. Its value depends on how grievance is composed. Where losses are material and the losers are the mobilizers, compensation addresses the grievance. Where the grievance is largely symbolic, or where the material loss falls on a broad group that compensation schemes do not reach, it does not.

\paragraph{Policy implications} Conditional on the model:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Whether caste stays a criterion matters more than any compensation scheme. An income filter inside caste categories achieves most of the income targeting that motivates the reform, at a small fraction of the unrest, and in IIT admissions it moves seats towards below-line SC and ST candidates rather than away from them.
\item A merged income-only pool is not a neutral re-labelling. It moves most reserved seats of below-line SC and ST candidates to below-line General candidates, and expanding capacity on the EWS model does not undo that.
\item If the income-only end state is the goal, phasing (grandfathering) and a process that changes what the reform signals (a commission, statutory guarantees) matter most, and they compound.
\item Suppression trades a lower peak for more deaths. Whether it also broadens protest depends on how deaths are received, which the model cannot tell.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Where protest would happen} The model places the reform's protest where SC and ST populations are largest, but the 2018 record shows that local organization can move a bandh's centre of gravity: Punjab, where the 2018 call began, reported far more events than its population share. The model's state distribution is a demographic baseline, not a prediction of where organizers will succeed.

\paragraph{Feasibility} The levers that work best are also the hardest to obtain or the furthest from the reform as proposed. Cross-party consensus on reservation is rare; L3 keeps caste as a criterion. The model treats political feasibility as exogenous.

\paragraph{What the platform can be used for} The same machinery answers questions beyond this paper's. The allocation model evaluates any pool design under the over-and-above rule, for example different pool sizes, caste sub-quotas within an income pool, or a creamy layer for SC and ST; the scenario catalogue takes new interventions as short functions; and the calibration machinery can absorb new episodes or new event data as they become available.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The reform's shock size is not identified.} The episodes identify how strongly the 2018 ruling threatened SC and ST recognition relative to the other episodes, not how strongly an income-only reform would. The grounded analysis assumes $R=1$ and backs the reform with opposition parties at the 2024 level. Table~\ref{tab:assumptions} shows that the level of protest moves by an order of magnitude across these assumptions; the ranking moves much less.
\item \textbf{The material weight is not identified.} The episodes involve no material change, so $w_m$ is assumed in both specifications. The break-even analyses bound how far it would have to move for the ranking to change.
\item \textbf{Event counts are an indirect measure.} GDELT counts reports, not people. The observation model is concave and anchored on two episodes with published crowd estimates, but the implied turnout for 2018 remains wide (\CalibTurnoutTwentyEighteenInterval{} lakh). The relevance labels were assigned from URL text by a single coder and have not been independently double-coded; the precision correction inherits that weakness. ACLED would provide an independent cross-check.
\item \textbf{Party backing and shock direction are coded, not estimated.} Each episode's party backing and the direction of its symbolic threat are judgements from reporting, held fixed in calibration; errors are absorbed by the estimated magnitudes.
\item \textbf{Intervention mappings are priors.} How much a commission, a guarantee or a filter reduces threat is not observed. The rank probabilities integrate over wide priors, but they cannot correct a mapping whose whole range is wrong.
\item \textbf{The allocation model covers one institution type.} Seat changes are estimated for IIT admissions, where rank lists are public. Government jobs, state institutions and medical admissions may differ; NEET failed our tail check. Family income is not observed per candidate and enters through priors constrained by aggregate evidence. Horizontal reservations and programme preferences are not modelled.
\item \textbf{Omitted structure.} Jati-level heterogeneity beyond two SC/ST tiers, state-level variation in income and organization, media dynamics, courts and electoral feedback are not modelled. State government alignment was coded (\path{data/derived/state_government_alignment.csv}) but is not used by the model.
\item \textbf{Generic dynamics.} Threshold cascades can produce heavy-tailed outcomes whatever their micro-foundations \citep{thron2015}. The episode tests, the structural ensemble and the separation of inputs from emergent effects are partial answers; they do not establish that the mechanisms are the right ones.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Replacing caste-based reservation with a single income test at \rupee8 lakh is a larger change than it appears. Under the actual rules, \EligibilityLose{} of Indians would lose eligibility and \EligibilityGain{} would gain it, and in a merged pool it moves most seats held by below-line SC and ST candidates to below-line General candidates, although those candidates keep their eligibility.

How much protest that would provoke is not something the available data can pin down. Event counts from past episodes identify the relative size of past shocks, not their headcounts, and out-of-sample predictions of deaths and geography are weak. What the model can do is compare designs on the same simulated worlds, and that comparison is robust in its main lines. Keeping caste as the qualifier with an income filter inside it cuts protest most, in every analysis in this paper, because it removes both the symbolic threat and, as the allocation model shows, the seat losses of the below-line majority. Compensating the direct losers and expanding seats on the EWS model do little. If the income-only end state is the goal, phasing and a process that changes what the reform means matter most, though the model cannot say which of the two matters more. Suppression costs lives in every specification, and its effect on the size of protest depends on how people respond to deaths, which no past episode identifies.

\paragraph{Future work} Four extensions would strengthen the evidence most. An independent event dataset (ACLED) would test the GDELT counts, the spatial predictions and, through its reported crowd sizes, the implied turnout of past bandhs; the pipeline for it is in place. A second, independent coding of the relevance audit would tighten the precision correction. Seat changes for government jobs and state institutions would test whether the IIT results generalize. And survey experiments on how an income filter, a commission or statutory guarantees change perceived threat would replace the priors over intervention effects with estimates.

\section{Ethics statement}
\label{sec:ethics}
The study uses no individual-level data. The population is synthetic and built from published aggregate shares; the JEE rank lists are used without roll numbers or names, and only rank and category are retained. Suppression tactics are included only to test whether they backfire, and they are not presented as options. Obfuscation, which is manipulating what citizens know about a reform, is deliberately excluded from the intervention set. The model is not a forecast of any actual policy. It is intended to inform the design of reforms that respect democratic contestation, not to help any actor suppress it. The state-level results describe where protest would concentrate; they are not, and should not be used as, a basis for pre-emptive policing.

\toprule
Element & Content\\
\midrule
Purpose & Compare designs of an income-only reservation reform by the street protest they would provoke; separate what data identify from what is assumed.\\
Entities & Agents (120{,}000, each about 12{,}200 people) with social group, SC/ST tier, income position, eligibility segment, identity strength and threshold; neighbourhoods of about 100 agents; districts and states (grounded); organizations and government as rules, not agents.\\
Scales & One step is a day; campaigns last 40 days; space is the 640 Census 2011 districts (grounded) or national (stylized).\\
Process overview & Each day: grievance, social pull and organizational push determine each agent's protest probability; decisions; fatigue; turnout updates; bandh calls and concession (grounded); deaths and the martyr effect (Section~\ref{sec:model}).\\
Design concepts & Emergence: turnout, bandh timing, concession, deaths. Adaptation: none (thresholds rise with fatigue). Interaction: neighbourhood and national social influence. Stochasticity: decisions, deaths, organization, world draws; paired seeds across scenarios. Observation: peak-day turnout, unique participants, protester-days, deaths, state and group shares, event counts through the observation model.\\
Initialization & Population from Census and NFHS shares with seed 7; parameters from the specification and, per run, a world draw (grounded: one retained calibration set).\\
Input data & Rank lists and allotments (allocation model), Census 2011 and NFHS-5 (population), GDELT event counts and reported deaths (calibration); none enter during a run.\\
Submodels & Grievance (Eq.~1), social pull (Eq.~2), decision (Eq.~3), bandh calls, concession and backlash, deaths; parameters in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}.\\
